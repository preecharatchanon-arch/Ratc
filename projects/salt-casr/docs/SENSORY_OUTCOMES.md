# Umami, saltiness and liking are separate product outcomes

[Project](../README.md) · [Research status](RESEARCH_STATUS.md) · [Saltiness test design](NEXT_TEST_DESIGN.md)

28 October 2026. Published measurements show that palatability can improve in a condition where a significant saltiness increase was not detected. This motivates a separate application question for savory prepared foods, including a possible future frozen-food formulation. Peptide-specific and frozen-food benefits remain untested here.

## A primary human example

Hayabuchi et al. (2020) studied 584 participants in a double-blinded randomized
evaluation of NaCl solutions with/without 0.3% MSG. In the 0.9% NaCl condition:

| Outcome, VAS mean±SE | Without MSG | With MSG | Source interpretation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Saltiness |73.8±0.6|72.3±0.7|No significant difference; reported P=0.3013|
| Palatability |40.7±0.7|51.6±0.8|Higher palatability reported with MSG|

These are published group summaries, not our measurements. The study added
an umami substance; these two scales do not independently establish an umami
intensity effect. It tests simple solutions, not peptides or frozen meals.
MSG contributes sodium, so equal NaCl is not equal total sodium. No significant
saltiness difference does not demonstrate equivalence or absence of a small
effect. The summaries alone do not supply paired covariance for a new test.

Primary: [Hayabuchi et al.,10.1038/s41440-020-0397-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41440-020-0397-1),
Figure2 and Tables2–3.

A separate consumer study in low-sodium chicken noodle soup reports increased
acceptance with MSG/IMP formulations. It supports a savory-food application
rationale, not a result for CaSR peptides or a proof of unchanged saltiness:
[Miyaki et al. (2016),10.1111/1750-3841.13195](https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.13195).

## Define the result before evaluating it

| Question | Required outcome | What it cannot substitute for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does it increase umami? | Separately elicited umami intensity | Consumer liking or saltiness |
| Does it improve kokumi? | Defined thickness, mouthfulness or continuity task | A basic-taste intensity or preference score |
| Does it taste better? | Controlled human liking, preference or palatability | Receptor activity or stronger umami alone |
| Does it enhance saltiness? | Separate saltiness contrast against matched background | Umami, liking, electronic-tongue output or combined intensity |
| Does it support sodium reduction? | Actual total-sodium basis plus specified sensory/product criterion | A fixed-NaCl enhancement result |
| Does it activate CaSR? | Defined functional receptor assay | Human perception, food suitability or consumer preference |

## A separate application opportunity

The product hypothesis would be: adding a specified peptide to a defined food
improves consumer liking by a useful amount, even when a meaningful saltiness
increase is not established. That could be worthwhile on its own. Commercial
advantage would need evidence against an appropriate existing formulation,
such as an MSG-based comparator where relevant, at defined total sodium and
practical doses. More intense umami does not guarantee greater liking.

For a frozen-food claim, the actual formulation, freezing/storage, preparation
and reheating conditions would belong in the tested domain. Bitterness,
off-taste, texture, aroma, stability, cost and food-use suitability would also
matter and require application-specific evidence. Receptor activity alone does not establish these properties or food-use suitability.

This opportunity has not replaced the current CaSR/saltiness test. A later
liking test needs its own hypothesis, competing explanations, actual eligible
data, independent units, primary comparator, practical margin, uncertainty
analysis and outcome-dependent decision before execution. Liking and saltiness require separate primary outcomes and evaluations.
